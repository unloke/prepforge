After implementing ancestor-node frequency weighting in Scout, we uncovered a deeper logic flaw.

## Symptoms

After analyzing 600 opponent games:
- Ranking positions 1-3: my advantage (as expected)
- Ranking positions 4-12: ALL opponent advantage (opponent >50% winrate, some -3.0cp for me)
- Maia3 confirms: front lines I'm better, back lines opponent is better

This is backwards — we should never recommend prep targets where opponent has the advantage.

## Diagnosis

The ancestor-frequency weighting change (commit 78ec80c) didn't cause this; it **exposed** a pre-existing bug.

**Before:** prefilter selected all lines opponent played (incl rare ones), ranked by Maia3 → rare lines mixed in, user didn't notice
**After:** prefilter selects lines opponent *frequently* played, ranked by (ancestorFreq × prefilterScore) → now the lines opponent **commonly plays and is good at** rank high → obviously wrong

## Root cause hypothesis

The prefilter's `scorePrefilterLine()` function evaluates **all** opponent's terminal lines with Stockfish, regardless of how often they play the ancestor branch. So:

**Example scenario:**
- Opponent's Najdorf (plays 12% of games): they're familiar, decent defense, Stockfish eval might show us +30cp but they actually play it well
- Rare sideline they played once (plays 0.1% of games): they blundered, Stockfish says +80cp

Now that we weight by ancestor frequency:
- Najdorf (12% freq × +30cp) = 3.6 → ranks high
- Rare line (0.1% freq × +80cp) = 0.08 → ranks low

But if opponent's actual winrate in Najdorf is 55% and in the rare line is 20%, we're recommending the wrong one.

## The real question

**Why would Stockfish evaluate a line opponent plays frequently (+55% winrate) as +30cp for us?** And vice versa, a line they rarely play (+20% winrate) as +80cp?

Possible explanations:
1. **Stockfish is wrong** — it's finding ghostly computer advantages that humans (opponent) don't fall for. Opponent's frequent play of Najdorf despite "computer disadvantage" suggests they have a defense Stockfish doesn't see.
2. **Our evaluation window is too shallow** — prefilter uses shallow Stockfish (3-5s), doesn't go deep enough to find opponent's defensive resources in main-system lines.
3. **Opponent's evolution** — they learned to handle their main systems better over time, but Stockfish eval doesn't account for temporal learning.
4. **Selection bias** — we're evaluating at MAX_PLIES=16 (move 8), but opponent's main systems might have safeguards *after* move 8 that Stockfish doesn't see from that shallow window.

## What should we do?

Two directions to investigate:

**A. Filter what prefilter analyzes**
Only run Stockfish on lines where:
- ancestorFrequency ≥ 1% (they play it often)
- OR opponent's empirical winrate in the ancestor < 45% (they actually struggle there)

This way, we don't waste eval on "they play it 50% of the time and win 60%, Stockfish says +50cp" lines.

**B. Trust opponent's actual performance, not Stockfish's eval**
For frequently-played lines, if opponent's actual winrate > 50%, downweight or skip them. Add a signal like:
```
prep_value = stockfish_advantage × (1 - min(1, opp_winrate / 50%))
// If they win 60%, multiply by (1 - 1.2) = negative, demote heavily
```

**C. Investigate the specific lines in your 600-game sample**
Pick one of the -3.0cp lines in position 12 and examine:
- How often does opponent play this ancestor branch?
- What's their actual winrate there?
- What does Stockfish think (at shallow vs deep)?
- What does Maia3 think?
- How does it compare to a front-ranked line (they play less, but Stockfish says +80cp)?

This will reveal whether the bug is in our eval, our frequency weighting, or our understanding of prep targets.

## My intuition

I suspect **B is closest**: opponent's actual performance in frequently-played lines is the signal we should trust. If they win >50% in a line they play 10% of the time, it's not a prep target — it's a line they're comfortable in. Stockfish eval is secondary.

The prefilter should be: "Find lines where opponent plays them often AND struggles there (low empirical winrate)."

Not: "Find lines where Stockfish thinks we're better, weight by how often they play."
