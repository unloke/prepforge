# Manual clarity review B — 13 actual studies

Reviewed 2026-11-03. **Manifest indices 13–25 inclusive, zero-based**: the 14th–26th entries of `corpus/manifest.json`. Read the local, original PGNs directly with python-chess: chapter sequence, introductions, move comments, alternative branches and custom-position exercise material. Inspected initial/middle/final chapters and additional substantive chapters per study; this is a qualitative reading, not an exhaustive verification of every line or a browser usability test.

**These labels concern instructional clarity within the stated role, not move strength, completeness or the author's chess ability. No engine result, annotation count or like count was used to assign them.** High likes can be external positive evidence; low/missing likes are unlabeled, never a reason for a negative. Do not fit these judgments to popularity or treat this small review as validated numeric ground truth.

## Findings by study

### Index 13 — tU7Nq5nE — The Alien and Martian Gambits

**Clarity: medium. Role: guided tactical gambit introduction. Confidence: medium.**

The introduction explicitly acknowledges risk and describes a route organized by opponents' common responses. Chapters 2–6 separate Alien responses; 7–9 introduce the Martian material. In chapter 6, the explanation after ...c5 connects it to ...Nc6; d5, ...Qxd5 and Bc4 then demonstrate the pin and ensuing fork. That is actual causal instruction, not merely congratulations.

The limitation is the recurring transition from a particular cooperative response to a claim of winning: chapter 3 develops Bd3, ...Bd6, castles, then ends in mate; chapter 9's ...e6 line finishes with a fork and a favorable numerical claim. The reader gets tactical examples more clearly than a guide to adapting when the opponent changes plan. Repeated promotion and praise also interrupt the route. **The author's acknowledgment of engine dislike does not make the teaching bad.**

Evidence: introduction and chapters 3, 6, 7, 9 in [original PGN](D:/auto_art/PrepForge%20Chess/research/atlas-quality/corpus/tU7Nq5nE.pgn).

### Index 14 — rZyGzD67 — Queen Attack guide

**Clarity: medium. Role: focused opening-response guide. Confidence: high.**

The first chapter explains the practical purpose of Qa4+: recover the pawn. Subsequent chapters separate Black's blocking choices. Chapter 4 explicitly sends the reader back to the ...Nc6 mainline after a transposition; chapter 6 explains why the pinned knight and absence of ...Nf6 allow e4. These are useful navigation and decision cues.

Some endpoints simply instruct development, with little explanation of subsequent choices. More concretely, chapter 7's note after ...Nf6 refers to a pawn on c3 blocking the b1 knight, although this line contains Black's ...c6. That square/side confusion makes the explanation hard to follow without reconstructing it. Chapter 6 gives win-rate percentages without population or sample provenance. The conclusion says certain omitted moves are bad instead of showing their handling. These are concrete clarity limitations, not an engine verdict.

Evidence: chapters 1, 4, 6–9 in [PGN](D:/auto_art/PrepForge%20Chess/research/atlas-quality/corpus/rZyGzD67.pgn).

### Index 15 — ztgulNGx — Ruy Lopez / Italian response guide

**Clarity: medium. Role: aggressive Black repertoire introduction. Confidence: medium.**

Accepted and declined responses are separately named. Chapter 4 warns against capturing the apparently free knight and supplies the Qh5+ consequence in a variation. Chapter 5 identifies activity and the open f-file as the resulting compensation; chapters 9–11 identify pins, pawn recovery and development. This gives learners more than a move sequence.

Other transitions amount to follow-the-arrow prompts or enthusiastic praise. The introduction's broad winning promise and repeated favorable endpoint evaluations are less informative than its concrete tactical explanations. It is a usable guided introduction, with weaker guidance when play leaves the illustrated sequence; not a poor study merely because its repertoire includes gambits.

Evidence: chapters 2, 4–6, 9–11 in [PGN](D:/auto_art/PrepForge%20Chess/research/atlas-quality/corpus/ztgulNGx.pgn).

### Index 16 — AKbQXhWw — White Repertoire

**Clarity: medium as a personal reference; weak as a standalone course. Role: personal repertoire notebook. Confidence: high.**

The organization is useful: overview, Queen's Gambit defenses, Indian defenses, Dutch, Englund, Catalan ideas and a mistakes chapter. Transposition notes connect branches. The overview gives a concrete setup plan of Qd2, long castling and Kb1 in one line.

The author also explicitly leaves work unfinished: the Slav chapter says **“TODO: learn and write down those variations”**; an overview Benoni/Benko note acknowledges missing knowledge. Several named defense chapters provide moves without explaining choices; the Benoni material even contains an unresolved question. This is an intelligible notebook for its owner, not evidence of a finished teaching route. Do not punish its personal-reference role simply for being concise.

Evidence: overview and chapters 3, 6, 9, 12–13 in [PGN](D:/auto_art/PrepForge%20Chess/research/atlas-quality/corpus/AKbQXhWw.pgn).

### Index 17 — 2MMWpcNI — Beginner-to-Grandmaster opening sampler

**Clarity: low as a standalone learning guide. Role: introductory sampler. Confidence: high.**

There are helpful beginnings: the Italian explanation connects Nf3 with development/central control, and the King's Gambit introduction describes a pawn offer and central aims. The negative judgment comes from specific confusing instruction. A chapter labeled Dutch starts from the initial position with **White** playing f4; the London material calls White's setup a defense and explains d4 through Black's bishop. Claimed audience tiers put London at an advanced rating range and a tactical attack at master level without explaining prerequisites or a learning progression.

Several chapters stop at a setup or an opponent's alleged blunder and celebrate the outcome. They do not explain how the reader should select among the listed openings or handle ordinary alternatives. These shortcomings can confuse a beginner independently of any engine assessment or popularity.

Evidence: chapters 2, 4, 6–8 in [PGN](D:/auto_art/PrepForge%20Chess/research/atlas-quality/corpus/2MMWpcNI.pgn).

### Index 18 — sEo8o4Rm — Nimzowitsch Defense guide

**Clarity: high in the main teaching chapters; uneven finish. Role: plan-based opening guide. Confidence: medium.**

The route progresses from starting moves to the main space-gaining response, accepted/declined Colorado, a King's Indian-style setup, and a dedicated h4 exception. Chapter 3 explains why d5 gains space/time, why the knight retreats, and why h4 needs different handling. Chapter 7 then supplies that exception: h4 would chase a knight on g6, so Black counters in the center. Chapter 8 identifies a Scotch transposition and directs readers to the relevant side branch. These connections teach recognition and adaptation.

Later generic chapter names and brief personal additions are less navigable. Some prose relies on claims about surprising opponents. The high judgment applies to the substantive introductory route, not a claim that every appended chapter is polished or that the opening is objectively best.

Evidence: chapters 2–8 and 12–16 in [PGN](D:/auto_art/PrepForge%20Chess/research/atlas-quality/corpus/sEo8o4Rm.pgn).

### Index 19 — GNlZ2WKx — London model games

**Clarity: high for a model-game collection. Confidence: medium.**

The introduction explains the positional/attacking symbols in chapter titles, so a reader can choose a thematic route. Chapter 2 connects the early bishop/knight move order to the queen's diagonal and later plans. Chapter 3 explains an opponent setup and offers alternative defenses; chapter 12 recalls the h4 idea from chapter 3. Chapter 25 discusses inaccuracies and their structural consequences instead of presenting every move as a recommendation.

It is a collection for learning recurring plans across games, not a complete reply policy. Some later material is sparsely explained and the last chapter has no apparent content. A move-error detector applied to both players' model-game moves would erase the educational distinction between a mistake and its explanation.

Evidence: introduction and chapters 2–3, 8, 12, 25, 30 in [PGN](D:/auto_art/PrepForge%20Chess/research/atlas-quality/corpus/GNlZ2WKx.pgn).

### Index 20 — KjivNw7F — London ideas

**Clarity: high for beginners. Confidence: high.**

This has the clearest learning route in this subset: numbered setup ideas, attacking examples, sacrifices, responses by Black's setup, an anti-London perspective, a quiz and a next-step chapter. Chapter 2 builds from d4/Bf4 to the pawn pyramid, development and f4. Chapter 9 explicitly contrasts a safer continuation with a riskier c4/Qb3 approach and explains the b7 target.

Chapter 5 states a combination's condition: it works only if Black recaptures with the bishop rather than the queen. Chapter 15 includes deliberately wrong alternatives with feedback; those are teaching devices. The final chapter openly limits the material's suitability for advanced players and explains how to examine model games and extend a repertoire. Promotions and some broad setup rules are distractions, but scope, prerequisites and progression are unusually clear.

Evidence: chapters 1–2, 5, 9, 14–16 in [PGN](D:/auto_art/PrepForge%20Chess/research/atlas-quality/corpus/KjivNw7F.pgn).

### Index 21 — 1gqDv9cx — Stafford investigation

**Clarity: high. Role: practical gambit/trap and refutation reference. Confidence: high.**

The opening note distinguishes playing the gambit from learning to defend against it, warns beginners against treating it as a sound foundation, and gives a practical context for the material. The table of contents separates accepted responses, declined play, explicitly labeled refutations and a reverse-gambit extension.

Chapter 16 explicitly changes to White's perspective and explains shutting down the bishop with d4. Chapter 17 explains the constriction and why Black cannot arrange long castling; chapter 18 frames new ideas as an attempted refutation with unresolved practical complications. Chapter 21 explains why opponents may decline the gambit and what transpositions result. This is strong evidence that losing-looking lines can improve a study's clarity by making its limitations explicit. Some dense tactical branches still demand prior knowledge.

Evidence: chapters 1, 16–18, 21–23 in [PGN](D:/auto_art/PrepForge%20Chess/research/atlas-quality/corpus/1gqDv9cx.pgn).

### Index 22 — 8eyxyvBh — Jerome exercises

**Clarity: low as a self-contained teacher; potentially useful as a drill supplement. Confidence: medium.**

The introduction clearly warns about the gambit's nature, and the line/exercise pairing makes the drill structure legible. The problem is instructional support: several exercises start at custom positions and supply solution moves with no explanation; chapter 6's exported exercise has a starting position but no continuation. Chapter 4 acknowledges a prepared defense with **“just hope for the best”**, rather than offering a plan or explaining the practical task. Other endpoints praise the position without identifying what the learner should retain.

This judgment is about incomplete feedback/context in the export, not the sacrifice itself. The PGN cannot establish how the live viewer hides solutions or handles the empty exercise; that part remains uncertain.

Evidence: chapters 1, 3–6, 9, 12–14 in [PGN](D:/auto_art/PrepForge%20Chess/research/atlas-quality/corpus/8eyxyvBh.pgn).

### Index 23 — dgUQNQ2B — Black d4 repertoire

**Clarity: medium as a lookup repertoire; weak as an explanatory course. Confidence: high.**

The chapter taxonomy maps recognizable White choices: Catalan variants, London/Jobava, Colle, Trompowsky/Torre and Nimzo branches. The supplied variations make it possible to find a response by opening name. Chapter 19 provides a useful conditional reason for delaying ...d5 until White commits to Nf3.

The introduction and many substantive chapters give no explanation of why the chosen replies fit together. The Trompowsky note changes a surprise recommendation without much rationale. There is no clear progression from basic plan to exception or practice. The limitation is a missing teaching layer, not necessarily a failure for someone maintaining already-understood lines.

Evidence: chapters 1–6, 8, 11, 13–15, 19 in [PGN](D:/auto_art/PrepForge%20Chess/research/atlas-quality/corpus/dgUQNQ2B.pgn).

### Index 24 — whCVdUeM — Stafford traps

**Clarity: medium as a compact trap collection. Confidence: medium.**

The introduction identifies the gambit's practical/dubious character and explicitly says each chapter teaches a trap, with a linked explanatory video. Named chapters emphasize recognizable bait and tactical consequences. Chapter 3 labels the natural-looking error and reveals the queen win; chapter 5 explains the surviving bishop's activity and interference with castling. Chapter 8 acknowledges that a spectacular king hunt may not resolve the position and asks for more analysis.

Some branches are mostly move sequences or punchlines; chapter 9's appeal to the engine is not an explanation of the mating mechanism. Without the linked video, the export teaches individual traps more clearly than robust play after an opponent avoids them. No external video was viewed, so its extra teaching value is unassessed.

Evidence: chapters 1, 3–5, 7–10 in [PGN](D:/auto_art/PrepForge%20Chess/research/atlas-quality/corpus/whCVdUeM.pgn).

### Index 25 — OnPMlzHT — Petrov guide

**Clarity: medium. Role: prompted introduction to named branches. Confidence: medium.**

The introduction establishes the starting moves and chapter 2 asks the learner to recall them. Later custom-position chapters isolate particular attacks. Chapter 4 points out the pin on the e4 knight before asking for a reply; chapter 6 asks how to drive away a centralized knight; chapter 5 explains the symmetrical pawn structure and moves toward development. Those are concrete prompts tied to the board.

Many answers supply praise or the next move rather than the reason. The introduction and conclusion also overstate the connection between choosing this defense and obtaining a draw. The branch labels are useful for orientation, but the export gives less support for independently choosing moves after each short sequence ends.

Evidence: chapters 1–8 in [PGN](D:/auto_art/PrepForge%20Chess/research/atlas-quality/corpus/OnPMlzHT.pgn).

## What this subset can inform

**Four high, seven medium, two low** provisional clarity judgments. Medium frequently means a useful reference with a limited teaching layer, not a deficient repertoire. The two low judgments rest on identifiable confusing guidance or missing exercise feedback; neither rests on obscurity, gambit identity or engine disagreement.

The most useful contrasts for designing a later rubric are: explained conditions versus isolated instructions; an intentional route versus a chapter list alone; clearly labeled example/refutation/quiz roles versus treating all stored moves as prescriptions; actionable scope limits versus unqualified promises; and specific feedback versus praise without a reason. These are observations from these sources, **not proposed metric weights or an automated scoring formula**. A short, expert-facing personal reference and a beginner course need different expectations.

High likes may corroborate usefulness after reviewing content. Low likes, a recently published study, or missing popularity metadata remain unknown. A future manual review should be blinded to likes where possible; popularity should not supply the negative class.

The frozen engine benchmark documents a failed joint path-score ranking (heldout AUC 0.357) and narrow same-engine mistake diagnostics. Those labels are not teaching-quality labels. No replacement engine benchmark was run after the task changed, and no v2 quality claim is made here.
